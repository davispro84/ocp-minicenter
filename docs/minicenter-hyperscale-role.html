OCP Mini-Center — Role vs. Hyperscale & Support Model

What this facility actually does, who it's for, why it isn't trying to be a small hyperscale site, and the specific ways it could support hyperscale operators rather than just compete with them.

POSITIONING — DRAFT v0.1 — 2026-10-06. Business framing built on already-locked engineering (Basis of Design, FMEA, weight/thermal budgets); the "support model" in Section 4 is new reasoning extending the existing target-customer work, not yet a checklist-tracked decision.

1. What It Does

A two-skid, crane-set, cable-terminated 520kW AI compute module: 4 OCP ORv3-class racks, N+1 Motivair MCDU-25 liquid cooling, standard 480V/415V 3-phase AC power, its own 600kW-class generator and adiabatic heat rejection — a complete, self-sufficient compute facility that needs a site with land, a power source (even a modest or intermittent one), and six to eight weeks of civil prep, not a utility interconnection or a multi-year construction schedule. It hosts standard commercial OEM AI hardware (GB200 NVL72-class racks) with no proprietary modification required.

3. Advantage vs. a Hyperscale Site

This isn't a smaller, cheaper hyperscale site — it's a structurally different product solving a different constraint. A hyperscale campus optimizes for capacity at scale once it's built; the Mini-Center optimizes for time-to-power-on and siting flexibility at the cost of absolute scale.

The honest framing: hyperscale wins on raw scale and unit economics once built. The Mini-Center wins on everything upstream of "once built" — the months-to-years where a hyperscale site doesn't exist yet, can't exist yet, or will never exist at all at that location.

4. What Support Role Could the Mini-Center Play for Hyperscale Operators?

Rather than only a standalone alternative, the architecture has real fit as a companion product to hyperscale operators and energy-portfolio owners who already run hyperscale-scale infrastructure elsewhere. None of this is locked design work yet — it's a reasoned extension of the existing target-customer analysis, flagged as such.

Bridge capacity
Deploy at a site where a hyperscale campus is permitted and under construction but not yet energized — fills the 24-36 month gap with real compute instead of idle land, then gets relocated or decommissioned once the permanent facility comes online.
Stranded-asset monetization for hyperscale-adjacent portfolios
A hyperscale operator or its power-portfolio partner often holds renewable PPAs with real curtailment — sited directly at the generation point, ahead of transmission buildout, rather than waiting for the grid to catch up.
Burst / overflow capacity
Short-notice demand spikes (a training run, a customer surge) that don't justify or can't wait for permanent campus expansion — portable capacity that can be added at an existing site's periphery.
Disaster recovery / business continuity
A truckable, self-sufficient compute module is a genuinely different failover story than a second data hall in the same region — can be positioned somewhere a primary facility's own outage or disaster doesn't reach.
Forward-deployed / edge inference
Pre-processing or inference close to remote data sources (oil & gas fields, mining operations, military or disaster-response sites) feeding a central hyperscale training cluster — reduces bandwidth/latency rather than backhauling raw data.
Low-risk pilot / testbed
A contained module is a lower-stakes place to pilot a new rack generation or cooling architecture before a full campus commitment — including, eventually, a future 800VDC-native rack generation once that becomes real commercial hardware (see the open question below).

Where this connects back to 800VDC

One specific version of the "pilot/testbed" role: if commercial AI racks eventually ship with native 800VDC input (an industry roadmap item — OCP's Mt. Diablo workstream, publicly discussed Nvidia next-generation rack plans — not available in commercial hardware today), the original Pod design's already-verified 800VDC engineering basis means a Mini-Center-class module could plausibly be the first real-world proving ground for that architecture at small scale, before a hyperscale operator commits campus-wide. That's a real option this project is positioned for, not a current capability — still gated on the same external dependency discussed previously: commercial rack vendors actually shipping 800VDC-native hardware.

5. What This Is Not

  • Not a replacement for hyperscale campuses at the multi-tens-of-megawatt training-cluster scale — 520kW is a deliberate ceiling, not a limitation being engineered away
  • Not yet engineered as a repeatedly-relocatable product (the bridge-capacity and disaster-recovery roles above are architecturally plausible given the truckable two-skid design, but re-deployment hasn't been designed or tested as its own use case)
  • Not a general-purpose enterprise or colocation offering — purpose-built for stranded power, not a shared-tenancy or carrier-neutral facility
