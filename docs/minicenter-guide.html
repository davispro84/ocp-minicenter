OCP Mini-Center — Plain-Language Design Guide

Written 2026-09-28 — a walkthrough for someone who wants the full story without wading through the engineering documents

What this project is, where it started, why we tore out a big piece of it and rebuilt that piece differently, who's actually going to buy this, and exactly how much work is left.

1. What Is This Project?

The idea: build a real, sellable "Mini-Center" — a shipping container packed with high-performance computer racks, its own power distribution, and its own cooling system — that can be trucked anywhere, craned into place next to whatever power source is available (a utility line, a generator, a solar farm, a stranded gas well), hooked up, and turned on. No years-long construction project, no massive data center building, no need for a small army of on-site staff.

The target: places a normal data center can't reach — rural areas, disaster zones, remote industrial sites, or anywhere that has power and land but not the roads, permits, or budget for a hyperscale build.

2. Where We Started

This project began life as something different: an existing piece of software that automates building a data center "pod" design in 3D modeling software (Revit), based on the most advanced power and cooling technology that companies like Google, Meta, and Microsoft use in their own private data centers. Think of it as a technical showcase — proving the automation tooling could correctly build a state-of-the-art design, not a commercial product.

When we decided to shrink that into a real, truck-deployable Mini-Center, we carried the same underlying technology forward:

PieceWhat it actually is, in plain terms
800-volt DC power busInstead of normal building electricity (AC), racks were fed a custom high-voltage DC current, converted by an external box (a "sidecar rectifier"). This is genuinely how Google/Meta/Microsoft power their own newest data centers internally.
Chilldyne vacuum cooling loopA liquid cooling system that runs under negative pressure (a vacuum) instead of normal positive pressure — clever because if a fitting leaks, it sucks air in instead of spraying coolant out. Very leak-safe, but a non-standard way of connecting to a rack.

Both are real, working, legitimate engineering — this is not a case of anyone building something fake or broken. The problem was somewhere else entirely.

3. The Reality Check

We asked a very simple question: if a real customer buys this Mini-Center, what happens when they try to put their own computer hardware into it?

Real companies (Dell, HPE, Supermicro) and NVIDIA's own reference designs for their latest AI chips (the GB200-class hardware everyone actually wants right now) are built to plug into standard building electricity and standard liquid-cooling connectors. They are not built to accept a custom 800-volt DC feed or a vacuum cooling loop. A customer who bought a $3 million rack of GPUs literally could not plug it into what we had designed — and trying to force it would void the manufacturer's warranty.

In other words: we had built something that only makes sense if the company selling the Mini-Center also builds its own custom computer hardware from scratch — which is realistically only something Google, Meta, or Microsoft can do at their scale. For anyone else, it's a beautifully engineered box that nothing else in the real world can plug into. An expert reviewer would rightly ask "why?" — and there wouldn't be a good answer for a company buying off-the-shelf hardware.

4. The Pivot

So we changed the one thing that was actually broken — not the ambition, not the toughness, not the goal — just the electrical and cooling connectors, so the Mini-Center becomes a universal host instead of a closed system:

V1 — HYPERSCALE SHOWCASEV2 — UNIVERSAL HOST (current)
Power Custom 800V DC bus, external rectifier box needed Standard building-style 3-phase AC (480V/415V) — same category of power any commercial building already has. The customer's own rack converts it internally, the same way it already does everywhere else.
Cooling connectors Chilldyne vacuum (negative pressure) — non-standard Standard positive-pressure connectors (the same kind NVIDIA/Dell/HPE racks already ship with)
Leak protection Built into the vacuum design itself Handled the way real data centers actually do it: a welded stainless steel drip pan under the whole row, plus a rope-like sensor that instantly shuts off the pumps if it detects liquid
Compute density kept? Yes — 130kW+ per rack (needs liquid cooling) Yes, unchanged. This pivot didn't make the box "less powerful" — it just made the same power level speak a language real hardware understands.
The important part: we didn't retreat to something simpler and weaker. We kept the high-performance liquid cooling and the extreme power density — we only fixed which plugs and connectors let real, purchasable hardware use it.

Why this is a genuine improvement, not just a compromise

  • A customer can actually buy this and use it tomorrow with hardware they already order from normal vendors — no waiting for us to build custom equipment, no convincing them to adopt a proprietary standard.
  • The real differentiator shifts to where it should be: not "we invented our own power standard," but "this box survives places nothing else can" — extreme heat, extreme cold, rough transport, and it keeps running without anyone on-site. That's genuinely hard to build and genuinely valuable. A proprietary power bus was never where the value was.
  • It didn't cost us anything real. The physics reason liquid cooling exists (130kW+ per rack is far beyond what any fan-based system can move) is completely unaffected by which connector standard we use.

5. Who's Actually Buying This Now

Working through this also surfaced something we hadn't pinned down clearly before: there are two different types of customers who might want this, and they don't need the same thing inside the box.

Primary Design (right now)Standard Tier (secondary, real, not urgent)
Who buys itAI compute operators — companies renting out GPU power to AI companies, deploying next to stranded/cheap power (natural gas, solar, wind) because waiting years for a utility hookup isn't an optionRural telecom, disaster response, remote mining/industrial sites, military forward-deployment, developing-region infrastructure
What they needExtreme-density GPU racks (130-190kW each) — genuinely can't get this any other wayModest, reliable general-purpose or light AI compute (10-30kW/rack) — could even be air-cooled
Why nowThis is where the money and urgency are right now — a real, currently booming market (companies like Crusoe Energy are already doing exactly this)This was the original inspiration for the whole project, and it doesn't go away — it's just not the first thing we're optimizing for

The good news: both customers can share almost the entire same box. The container shell, the electrical room, the climate-hardening, the structural design for rough transport — all identical. The only thing that changes between the two is what kind of rack and cooling gets bolted into the compute room. That's a real product-line strategy, not two separate designs.
